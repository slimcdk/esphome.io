Read and control Mastervolt equipment — battery blocks, chargers, inverters, displays — from
ESPHome over a CAN transceiver.

MasterBus is a closed protocol running at 250 kbit/s on CAN. The wire format used here was
recovered by watching a live installation and checking every step against the vendor's own
library. Nothing in this component guesses at a frame it has not seen.

> [!WARNING]
> This component can write to the bus, and on a real installation those fields open battery
> contactors and switch chargers. Read [Writing to the bus](#writing-to-the-bus) before you
> configure a `switch`, `button` or `number`.

## Setup

The component sits on top of the standard [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) component, so any CAN
platform ESPHome supports will do.

### A configuration to start from

You do not need to know anything about your bus to begin. This is complete: paste it, set the two
pins, flash, and read the log. It transmits one broadcast, lists every device that answers, and
prints the configuration for what it finds.

```yaml
esp32:
  board: esp32dev
  framework:
    type: esp-idf

logger:
  # The canbus component logs one line per frame. A busy MasterBus carries about 200 a second,
  # which is far more than a serial log can carry, so mute it.
  logs:
    canbus: WARN

canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    id: mb_can
    tx_pin: GPIO5     # to the transceiver's TX / D
    rx_pin: GPIO4     # to the transceiver's RX / R
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
    rx_queue_len: 32

masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
  scan: true
```

Turn `scan` off again once you have what you need — it walks four tabs of every group of every
device and takes minutes. Then add `devices:` and the entities the scan printed.

If your equipment is one of the products listed under
[Configurations for known equipment](#configurations-for-known-equipment), you can take the entity
blocks from there instead and only change the addresses.

### Configuring the CAN bus

```yaml
canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    id: mb_can
    tx_pin: REPLACEME
    rx_pin: REPLACEME
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
    rx_queue_len: 32
```

- **bit_rate** (**Required**): MasterBus runs at **250 kbit/s**. No other rate will work.
- **mode** (*Optional*): `NORMAL` lets the node transmit, which is what polling and writing need.
  Use `LISTENONLY` for a first look at a bus you would rather not touch; entities still update
  from what other devices ask for.
- **rx_queue_len** (*Optional*): a busy MasterBus carries about 200 frames a second. A queue of 32
  or more keeps frames from being dropped during a slow loop.

> [!NOTE]
> The `canbus` component logs one line per frame at DEBUG. At 200 frames a second that is far more
> than a 115200 baud serial log can carry. Add `logs: {canbus: WARN}` to your
> [Logger](/components/logger/) unless you are specifically debugging the bus.

### Using an external CAN controller

Not every board has a CAN peripheral. ESPHome offers exactly two platforms, and which one you can
use is decided by your MCU:

| Platform | Interface | Works on |
|---|---|---|
| [`esp32_can`](/components/canbus/esp32_can/) | built in | the ESP32 family only |
| [`mcp2515`](/components/canbus/mcp2515/) | SPI | anything ESPHome runs on |

There is **no I2C or UART CAN platform** in ESPHome. If your board has no CAN peripheral, SPI and
an MCP2515 is the route.

The MCP2515 is a full CAN 2.0B controller, so it handles everything MasterBus needs: 250 kbit/s is
available on all four supported crystal frequencies, and extended identifiers — which every
MasterBus message uses — are supported in both directions.

```yaml
spi:
  clk_pin: GPIO18
  mosi_pin: GPIO23
  miso_pin: GPIO19

canbus:
  - platform: mcp2515
    id: mb_can
    cs_pin: GPIO5
    clock: 8MHZ       # must match the crystal on your module
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
```

Three things catch people out:

**The `clock` must match the crystal actually fitted to your module**, not what the listing said.
Boards ship with 8 MHz and 16 MHz crystals and look identical; the part is marked on the can. Get
it wrong and the bit rate is wrong by the same ratio, which looks like a dead bus rather than like
a misconfiguration.

**Most cheap MCP2515 modules are 5 V parts.** They pair the controller with a TJA1050 or MCP2551
transceiver and expect 5 V logic. On a 3.3 V MCU the SPI lines need level shifting, and the
transceiver itself will not run from 3.3 V. Modules sold as 3.3 V, or ones where you replace the
transceiver with an SN65HVD230, avoid the problem.

**The module already contains a transceiver.** The [Hardware](#hardware) section below is written
around wiring a bare transceiver to an MCU's own CAN pins; with an MCP2515 module that part is done
for you. Everything from [Finding CAN_H and CAN_L](#finding-can_h-and-can_l) onwards still applies
— termination, grounding and the connector pinout do not care which controller you chose.

> [!WARNING]
> **The MCP2515 holds only two received frames.** ESPHome polls it from the main loop rather than
> using its interrupt pin, and empties both buffers on each pass. On a quiet bus that is ample. A
> MasterBus carrying around 200 frames a second delivers one every five milliseconds, so two slots
> is a thin margin against a main loop that can take ten milliseconds or more when something else
> is busy — and anything arriving while both are full is lost. By comparison `esp32_can` buffers in
> software, and `rx_queue_len: 32` holds well over a hundred milliseconds of the same traffic.
>
> Lost frames degrade freshness rather than break anything: a missed answer is asked for again on
> the next `update_interval`. How much it matters in practice has not been measured here.

### Configuring the hub

```yaml
masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
```

- **id** (*Optional*, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): the hub's ID, so entities can
  reference it.
- **canbus_id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): the
  [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) component to listen on.
- **scan** (*Optional*, boolean, default `false`): ask every device on the bus to announce itself
  at boot and print a pasteable configuration. See [Finding your
  equipment](#finding-your-equipment).
- **log_all_frames** (*Optional*, boolean, default `false`): log every MasterBus frame at DEBUG,
  with the message type and address already decoded. For protocol work, not for normal use.
- **on_unknown_frame** (*Optional*, [Automation](/guides/automations#automation)): fires for any
  frame whose message type this component cannot name. See
  [`on_unknown_frame`](#on_unknown_frame).
- **devices** (*Optional*, list): the equipment you want to talk to. See below.

Several hubs may be configured, one per CAN bus.

### Configuring devices

Every entity belongs to a device, and a device is one physical unit addressed by the identifier it
announces on the bus.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_battery
      device: 0x6D56EA
      timeout: 60s
```

- **id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): referenced by entities through
  `masterbus_device_id`.
- **device** (**Required**, hex int): the device's MasterBus address, `0x0` to `0x7FFFFF`. The
  extended CAN identifier gives the address 23 bits; the rest carries the message type.
- **timeout** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time), default `60s`): how long
  without a frame before the device counts as offline and its entities go unavailable.
- **on_online** / **on_offline** (*Optional*, [Automation](/automations/actions/)): see
  [Automations](#automations).

## Finding your equipment

You do not need to know your addresses in advance. Set `scan: true`, flash, and read the log:

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  scan: true
```

The scan sends a node request, then walks every group of every device that answers — on all four
tabs — reading each field's number, display type, name, unit and range. It prints the result as
configuration you can paste:

```txt
[I][masterbus.scan]: Scan found device 0x6D56EA
[I][masterbus.scan]: Walking 7 devices across all four tabs for their fields.
[I][masterbus.scan]: sensor:
[I][masterbus.scan]:   # device 0x6D56EA, monitoring tab, group 1: Battery
[I][masterbus.scan]:   - platform: masterbus
[I][masterbus.scan]:     masterbus_device_id: mb_device_6D56EA
[I][masterbus.scan]:     param: 139
[I][masterbus.scan]:     name: "Battery"
[I][masterbus.scan]:     update_interval: 10s
[I][masterbus.scan]:     unit_of_measurement: "V"
[I][masterbus.scan]:     # range 0 to 600 step 0.01
```

The group name is printed as a comment above each group's fields, and it matters: field names on
this equipment are short and repeat. A battery block reports three separate fields all called
`Battery` — voltage, current and temperature — and it is the group that tells them apart.

**A field number only means something together with its tab.** Field 9 is `AC Input` current on a
MassCombi's monitoring tab; the same number on another tab is another field entirely. So the scan
prints a `tab:` key for everything outside monitoring, and names the tab in the group comment.

Three of the four tabs can be read. The alarm tab cannot, and the scan says so where it matters:

```txt
[I][masterbus.scan]:   # device 0x6D56EA, alarm tab, group 0: unnamed
[I][masterbus.scan]:   # this tab's values cannot be read yet - its data message is not decoded
[I][masterbus.scan]:   # device 0x6D56EA, configuration tab, group 0: Settings
[I][masterbus.scan]:   # this tab's data message is derived, not measured - check what it returns
```

See [Tabs](#tabs) for what each of those two lines means.

The bus does not report whether a field can be written, so the scan always suggests the read-only
platform of a pair: a `sensor` where a `number` may be possible, a `binary_sensor` where a
`switch` may be. Promote it yourself if the field turns out to be writable.

> [!TIP]
> Scanning transmits, so it needs `mode: NORMAL`. It walks four tabs for every group of every
> device, so it takes a good many minutes on a full installation. Turn it off again once you have
> your configuration.

## Entities

### Common options

Every platform below accepts these, in addition to its own base schema:

- **masterbus_device_id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): which device this
  field belongs to.
- **param** (**Required**, hex int, `0x0`–`0xFFFF`): the field number.
- **tab** (*Optional*, default `monitoring`): `monitoring`, `alarm`, `history` or `configuration`.
  Three of the four can be read; only `monitoring` can be written. See [Tabs](#tabs).
- **value_type** (*Optional*): how to read the 32 bits the device sends. Each platform accepts only
  the types it can render, and defaults sensibly, so you rarely need to set this.
- **update_interval** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time)): see [Polling and
  update_interval](#polling-and-update_interval). **Left unset the entity never transmits.**
- **timeout** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time)): how long without a value
  before this one entity goes unavailable. Unset, it follows its device. Use it for a field that
  changes rarely by nature, such as a relay, sitting beside fields that update every second.

### `sensor`

Numeric readings. Accepts `value_type` of `float` (default), `boolean` or `list_option`.

```yaml
sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_battery
    param: 139
    name: Block voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 2
```

A field that reports "not a number" publishes as unavailable rather than as zero.

### `binary_sensor`

A boolean field, read only.

```yaml
binary_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 21
    name: Charger running
    update_interval: 1min
```

### `switch`

A boolean field you can also set.

```yaml
switch:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 21
    name: Charger on
```

The switch publishes nothing when you operate it. It follows what the device reports afterwards,
so it never shows a state the equipment did not confirm. Give it an `update_interval`, or make
sure something else on the bus polls the field, or it will never show a state at all.

### `button`

Fires an event field once. Buttons take no `update_interval` — there is nothing to read back.

```yaml
button:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_battery
    param: 117
    name: Close relay
```

### `number`

A numeric field you can set. `min_value`, `max_value` and `step` are required; a scan prints the
device's own range as a comment.

```yaml
number:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 20
    name: Charger max current
    min_value: 0
    max_value: 100
    step: 1
    unit_of_measurement: "%"
```

### `select`

A list field. The options are the device's own, listed in the order it numbers them, because the
reported index picks the entry.

```yaml
select:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 18
    name: Charger stage
    options: ["Bulk", "Absorption", "Float"]
```

> [!NOTE]
> Get the order from a scan, not from guesswork. An index the list does not cover publishes as
> unavailable and logs a warning.

### `text_sensor`

Accepts `value_type` of `text` (default), `time` or `date`.

```yaml
text_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 24
    name: Charger battery name
    update_interval: 5min
```

A **text** field does not answer with text. It answers with the number of an entry in the device's
own string table, and the component reads that entry to get the string — two exchanges rather than
one. A field whose entry the device does not hold publishes as unavailable.

A **time** field counts seconds, and publishes as `h:mm:ss`. The same field type serves both a
clock and a countdown — a shunt's `Time` is a time of day, its `Remaining` is a span of hours — so
the hour is not folded into a day and a long span reads as `160:52:00`.

A **date** field packs the calendar into one number, and publishes as `YYYY-MM-DD`.

A number that cannot be either — negative, or larger than the format holds — publishes as
unavailable and logs a warning, rather than being truncated into something that looks plausible.

## Tabs

A MasterBus device organises what it exposes into four tabs, and `tab:` picks which one an entity
reads. The same field number means different things on different tabs, so the tab is part of a
field's address, not a filter.

| Tab | Read | Write |
|---|---|---|
| `monitoring` (default) | yes, measured | yes, measured |
| `configuration` | yes, derived | no |
| `history` | yes, derived | no |
| `alarm` | no | no |

**Measured** means frames of that shape were captured on a live bus and checked against what the
vendor's own software reported for the same field. **Derived** means the message number follows
from the vendor's own ordering — six of its twelve entries are pinned by captured traffic, and
these follow the same sequence — but no answer on that tab has been seen. The payload is assumed to
be monitoring's, a field number and a 32-bit float. An answer that is not that length is dropped
rather than guessed at, so a wrong assumption shows up as a value that never arrives, not as a
wrong reading.

The `alarm` tab is different in kind. The vendor gives every other tab a message for reading a
value; for alarms it files that message with the broadcast messages instead, and its numbering is
not known. So an alarm field can be discovered by a scan — its number, name, unit and range are all
readable — and still not be readable as a value. An entity on the `alarm` tab is accepted in
configuration and refused at runtime with a warning.

Writing stays on `monitoring` alone. The write frame, and the second frame that must follow it,
were captured on monitoring event fields and nowhere else. The configuration tab is exactly where a
wrong frame would change a charger's voltage, so it waits for a write to be watched on that tab.

## Polling and `update_interval`

A MasterBus device answers a field **only when asked**. Nothing arrives unprompted, so an entity
with no `update_interval` never transmits and stays silent — which is deliberate, so that a first
configuration on someone's boat or battery bank does nothing until asked.

The unusual part: **an answer meant for someone else counts.** MasterBus answers are broadcast, so
a display panel or another bridge asking the same device for the same field updates your entity
too. When that has already happened within the interval, the request is skipped.

So `update_interval` is an upper bound on how stale a value may get, and an upper bound on your own
traffic — not a fixed transmit rate. On a bus where a display panel already polls at 1 Hz, a node
configured with 35 entities at `10s` was measured transmitting **nothing at all** for the fields the
panel covered, while every entity kept updating. For a field nobody else wants, the same node polls
at exactly the configured interval.

## Writing to the bus

Writing works, and on real equipment it does real things. Three properties of the protocol are
worth knowing before you wire a button into an automation.

**There is often no state to read back.** Fields like `Close relay` and `Open relay` are *events*:
firing one sends the command, and reading the field afterwards always returns false. It reports the
last command, not the contactor's position. The only evidence a command took effect is some other
field — the block's own current, say.

**A write that reaches the bus is not a command that took effect.** Equipment can be shared between
devices, and then only one of them accepts the command for it. On the installation this component
was developed against, two battery blocks share one contactor: either one opens it, but only one of
the two closes it again. Correctly formed writes to the other block changed nothing at all, with no
error anywhere.

**Nothing on the bus says which fields are writable.** No read-only flag has been found among a
field's properties, so a write to a field that does not take one looks exactly like a write that
worked. A wrong `param` fails silently.

This component therefore does not retry, does not publish optimistically, and does not decide which
device to address on your behalf. Which device owns what is a property of your installation, not of
the protocol. Read the result back and build the retry logic you need on top.

## Automations

### `on_online`

Fires the first time a device answers after being offline.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_battery
      device: 0x6D56EA
      on_online:
        - logger.log: "Battery bank answered"
      on_offline:
        - logger.log: "Battery bank stopped answering"
```

### `on_offline`

Fires when nothing has been heard from the device for its `timeout`.

### `on_unknown_frame`

Fires for any frame whose message type this component cannot name. It sits on the hub rather than
on a device, and deliberately: the frames worth catching here come from equipment that may never
have been declared, which is exactly what a device-level trigger cannot see.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  on_unknown_frame:
    - logger.log:
        format: "undecoded message 0x%02X from device 0x%06X, %u bytes"
        args: ["type", "device", "x.size()"]
```

Three values are available: `x` is the payload as a `std::vector<uint8_t>`, `type` is the six-bit
message type, and `device` is the 23-bit address it came from or was addressed to.

This is an instrument, not a feature. The protocol has messages the vendor names and nobody here
has numbers for — an alarm's value is one of them — and the only way to learn where they sit is to
catch one. On a healthy bus it never fires.

If you are trying to catch something rare on equipment you cannot sit and watch, push it somewhere
that keeps it rather than writing it to a log:

```yaml
    - homeassistant.event:
        event: esphome.masterbus_unknown
        data:
          type: !lambda return format_hex(type);
          payload: !lambda return format_hex_pretty(x);
```

Nothing is compiled in unless you configure the trigger.

## Configurations for known equipment

Each section below is a paste-ready configuration for one kind of Mastervolt device. Change the
`device:` address to your own — [Finding your equipment](#finding-your-equipment) prints it — and
leave everything else as it stands.

**Field numbers are not standard across Mastervolt equipment.** The same number means different
things on different products: field 9 is `AC Input` current on a MassCombi and `Time` on a shunt.
A configuration is only valid for the product it was taken from. If yours is not listed, scan it —
the scan prints blocks like these for whatever answers.

Only the first section carries the `canbus:` and `logger:` stanzas; the rest assume them, and
several devices share one hub. Mixing equipment means one `masterbus:` block with each device
listed under `devices:`.

> [!NOTE]
> The battery block section was measured on a live installation and is in daily use. The MassCombi
> and shunt sections were rebuilt from a capture of a **different** installation taken in 2022: the
> field numbers, names, units and ranges are the devices' own, read out of their string tables, but
> those two configurations have not themselves been run. Check the values against your equipment's
> own display before trusting them.

### Lithium battery block

Blocks report twice: a **cluster** group carrying the bank as a whole, which every block answers
with the same numbers, and a **battery** group carrying that one block's own readings. So the bank
totals are read from any single block, and the per-block entities are repeated for each.

```yaml
canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    id: mb_can
    tx_pin: GPIO20
    rx_pin: GPIO21
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
    rx_queue_len: 32

logger:
  logs:
    canbus: WARN

masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_bat1
      device: 0x6D56EA
      on_offline:
        - logger.log: "BAT 1 stopped answering"
    - id: mb_bat2
      device: 0x6D4ECC

sensor:
  # Group 0, the cluster: the bank as a whole. Read from one block only - every block answers
  # these with the same numbers, so polling them all is the same value six times.
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 0
    name: Bank state of charge
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: "%"
    device_class: battery
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 1
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 1
    name: Bank voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 2
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 2
    name: Bank current
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: A
    device_class: current
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 2
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 3
    name: Bank capacity consumed
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: Ah
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 1
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 5
    name: Bank temperature
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: "°C"
    device_class: temperature
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 1

  # Group 1, the battery: this block alone. Repeat the five for every further block, changing
  # only masterbus_device_id and the names.
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1
    param: 136
    name: BAT 1 state of charge
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: "%"

  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_bat1

```

> [!WARNING]
> Fields 117 and 119 operate the contactor that ties the block to the bank. Opening one
> disconnects a real battery, and nothing asks twice. Neither field reports the contactor's
> position — the block's own current is the only evidence a command took effect. On the
> installation this was measured on the blocks are paired and one contactor serves each pair:
> either block opens it, only the lower-numbered one closes it. See
> [Writing to the bus](#writing-to-the-bus).

### MassCombi inverter/charger

Four groups: the state fields, the battery side, AC in and AC out.

```yaml
masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_combi
      device: 0x3AF412

sensor:
  # Group 1: the battery side.
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_combi
    param: 6
    name: Battery voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 2
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_combi
    param: 7
    name: Battery current
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: A
    device_class: current
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 1

  # Group 2: shore or generator in.
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_combi
    param: 8
    name: AC input voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 0
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_combi
    param: 9
    name: AC input current
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: A
    device_class: current
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 1

  # Group 3: the inverter output.
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_combi
    param: 10
    name: AC output voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
```

## Hardware

Everything here is about getting an MCU physically onto the bus. None of it is specific to
MasterBus's use of CAN — it is the same job as putting a board on any 250 kbit/s CAN segment, with
one complication: the connector carries power as well, and its pinout is not published.

### What you need

- **A CAN transceiver with 3.3 V logic.** The MCU's CAN peripheral gives you logic-level TX and RX;
  the transceiver turns that into the differential pair. An **SN65HVD230** or **TJA1051T/3** works
  directly with an ESP32. Avoid the **MCP2551** and similar 5 V-only parts — their logic levels do
  not match a 3.3 V MCU without level shifting.
  If you are using an [MCP2515 module](#using-an-external-can-controller) you already have one on
  the board and can skip this; check which transceiver is fitted, because the common modules pair
  the controller with a 5 V part.
- **A way into the bus.** MasterBus uses RJ45 connectors and daisy-chains between devices. An
  **RJ45 male breakout board** is the least invasive route: it plugs into a spare port on a
  MasterBus hub and brings the eight conductors out to screw terminals, with nothing cut or
  soldered.
- **A multimeter.** Not optional — see below.

### Finding CAN_H and CAN_L

> [!CAUTION]
> **Do not trust a pinout table you found online, including this one.** The MasterBus RJ45 pinout
> is not published by Mastervolt. The community sources that do exist **disagree with each other**,
> and the cable carries **+12 V**. Wiring a transceiver to the power pins destroys it.

Identify the pair yourself, with the installation **powered down**:

1. Measure resistance between conductors, pair by pair. The CAN pair reads about **60 Ω** — two
   terminators in parallel at the ends of the chain. Nothing else on the connector will read that.
2. Power the bus back up and confirm with a voltmeter. The supply pair sits at about **12 V**.
   CAN_H and CAN_L both idle near **2.5 V** relative to the bus ground, moving a volt or so apart
   when traffic flows.

On the installation this component was developed against, the CAN pair turned out to be the
**orange** and **orange/white** conductors. Yours may differ. Measure.

### Wiring the transceiver

Three connections to the MCU, two to the bus, and the standby pin:

| Transceiver | Connects to | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `CTX` / `TXD` | MCU CAN transmit pin | |
| `CRX` / `RXD` | MCU CAN receive pin | |
| `VCC` | MCU 3.3 V | not 5 V, for an SN65HVD230 |
| `GND` | MCU ground **and** MasterBus ground | see [Grounding](#grounding-and-isolation) |
| `CANH` | MasterBus CAN_H | the pair you measured |
| `CANL` | MasterBus CAN_L | |
| `Rs` / `S` | **ground** | high-speed mode |

Then name the two MCU pins in your `canbus:` block:

```yaml
canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    tx_pin: GPIO20   # to CTX
    rx_pin: GPIO21   # to CRX
    bit_rate: 250kbps
```

On an ESP32 the CAN controller can be routed to most GPIOs, so pick any free pair — but avoid the
chip's strapping pins, and anything already taken by flash, PSRAM or USB. The reference setup uses
an **ESP32-C6** with GPIO20 and GPIO21.

> [!WARNING]
> The `Rs` pin is the single most misleading fault on these modules. Held high it puts the
> transceiver in standby, where the **receiver still works and the driver does not**. The node then
> reads the bus perfectly and every transmit fails with `ERROR_ALLTXBUSY` — which looks exactly
> like a dead chip or a software bug. Tie `Rs` to ground.

### Termination

A CAN segment is terminated with 120 Ω at each **end**, and nowhere else. MasterBus is a daisy
chain that Mastervolt already terminates at both ends with its own terminator plugs, so a board
tapping into a spare hub port sits on a **stub** and must not add a terminator of its own.

**Most SN65HVD230 breakout modules ship with a 120 Ω resistor fitted.** Leaving it in place makes
three terminators: the bus drops from about 60 Ω to about 40 Ω and starts throwing form and stuff
errors. Remove the resistor, or open the module's jumper if it has one.

Check with everything powered down, measuring across CAN_H and CAN_L:

| Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ~60 Ω | Two terminators. Correct. |
| ~120 Ω | One terminator. One end is unterminated. |
| ~40 Ω | Three. Remove yours. |

> [!NOTE]
> If it is missed, the symptom is not obvious: the controller initialises cleanly and then either
> receives nothing or goes bus-off.

### Grounding and isolation

The transceiver needs a ground reference shared with the bus. Whether that is safe to make
directly depends on your installation.

**Measure it.** With everything powered down, measure the resistance between MasterBus 0 V and the
ground of anything else the MCU touches — another CAN bus, a USB host, a mains-powered supply.

- **Bonded (below about 1 Ω):** the grounds are already common. A bare transceiver adds no new
  path, and an SN65HVD230 is fine.
- **Not bonded:** connecting them creates a new current path between two systems that were
  separate, which on a battery installation can mean a ground loop carrying real current. Use an
  **isolated** transceiver such as an **ISO1042**, with an isolated supply for its bus side.

If the MCU also sits on a second CAN bus — an inverter link, say — check that pair too. Two bare
transceivers on one board bond both buses to each other through the MCU's ground.

### Powering the board

The MasterBus cable carries about 12 V, and it is tempting to run the MCU from it. Two things to
weigh before you do:

- It is the installation's supply, fused and budgeted for MasterBus devices. An ESP32 with WiFi
  draws short peaks of a few hundred milliamps, which is small but not nothing.
- A separate supply avoids that entirely, but then the grounding question above becomes the one you
  have to answer.

Either way the transceiver's ground must reference the bus.

## Troubleshooting

Messages below are the ones the component and the CAN layer actually print, so you can search for
what you are looking at.

### `send to extended id=0x… failed with error 2!`

The name behind error 2 is `ERROR_ALLTXBUSY`, and it is misleading: the ESP32 driver reports it for
every transmit failure, not only for a full queue. Read it as "the send did not happen", then work
out which of two very different faults you have. **What separates them is whether anything is still
being received.**

*Values keep arriving, only sending fails.* The receive path works and the transmit path does not.
Check the transceiver's `Rs`/standby pin, then swap the transceiver — this exact symptom has turned
out to be a module fault.

*Nothing arrives either.* The controller has gone bus-off. It enters that state after about thirty
consecutive failed transmissions of one frame — roughly twenty milliseconds — and then disconnects
itself from the bus entirely, so reception stops too and every entity goes unavailable. **Nothing
in ESPHome brings a controller out of bus-off**: recovery needs an explicit call, an asynchronous
wait and a second call, and none of them happen. Only a reboot restores it.

A channel that was never wired looks identical: a controller transmitting where nothing can
acknowledge it reaches bus-off in milliseconds and stays there. If a channel is silent in both
directions, suspect the wiring before the driver.

### `Scan heard nothing. Check wiring, bit rate and termination`

Nothing at all is being received. Check the bit rate is `250kbps` first — it is the single most
common cause and the symptom is total silence rather than corruption. Then measure 60 Ω across
CAN_H and CAN_L with the bus powered down. Then the wiring.

### `Cannot poll field N on the alarm tab: the message that carries its values is not decoded`

Expected, and not a misconfiguration. The alarm tab can be discovered but not read. See
[Tabs](#tabs).

### `Cannot write field N on the … tab: only monitoring writes have been verified`

Reading a tab and writing it are separate questions. Only `monitoring` writes have been captured
and checked. See [Tabs](#tabs).

### `Wrote field N but could not send the frame that follows it`

The value went out but the frame that must follow it did not, so the device may have taken the
write, ignored it, or half-applied it. Read the field back before assuming anything. This is a
transmit failure like any other — see error 2 above.

### `Could not put the node request on the bus`

The scan could not transmit, so it falls back to listening for devices that announce themselves.
You will still find equipment, but only what speaks unprompted. Scanning needs `mode: NORMAL`.

### `Scan is full at N devices`

More devices answered than the scan can hold. The rest are not listed. Raise the limit or scan a
smaller bus.

### `Maximum number of esp32_can components created already`

More `esp32_can` blocks than the chip has CAN controllers. An ESP32-C6 has two; most other ESP32
variants have one. Use an [MCP2515](#using-an-external-can-controller) for a further bus.

### `canbus took a long time for an operation`

The frame drain and everything it triggers ran longer than the loop allows. A verbose log over a
slow serial port is the usual cause: the logger blocks until the port accepts the bytes. Mute the
frame log, lower the log level, or read the log over the API.

### Entities stay unavailable

Either the device is not answering — check the address against a scan — or nothing is asking. An
entity with no `update_interval` transmits nothing and only updates when something else on the bus
asks for the same field.

### One entity goes unavailable while the rest of its device is fine

It has a `timeout` of its own and the field has not been answered within it. Either the field
changes more rarely than the timeout allows, or nothing is polling it.

### A write is accepted but nothing happens

See [Writing to the bus](#writing-to-the-bus). Check whether another device shares the equipment
you are commanding, and that the field takes a write at all — nothing on the bus says which do.

### The log is missing lines or is full of half-lines

The serial log cannot keep up with a busy bus. Mute the frame log with `logs: {canbus: WARN}`,
raise the baud rate, or read the log over the API instead. Two readers on the same serial port also
produce interleaved half-lines that look like corruption but are not.

## Limitations

- The `alarm` tab cannot be read, and nothing but `monitoring` can be written. See [Tabs](#tabs).
- A list field reports its index, not its entry. To get names you supply the list yourself, in the
  device's own order.
- This component does not read whether a field is writable, nor a device's article number, product
  name or serial number. The bus carries at least some of it — the vendor's own software reports an
  article number, and names a "writeable" property — but which message carries what has not been
  identified. A device's status here comes from whether it answers, not from what it reports.
- A CAN controller that goes bus-off never recovers on its own. This is a property of the
  underlying [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) platform rather than of this component, but it is what
  a MasterBus node looks like when it dies: every entity unavailable, the bus itself healthy, and
  only a reboot fixes it. See [Troubleshooting](#troubleshooting).
- Firmware update over MasterBus is out of scope, and the bootloader tab is deliberately absent.
- The component carries no table of device types, article numbers or field layouts, and never
  infers one. What a field means is a property of your equipment. The configurations above are
  documentation of what has been observed, not something the component knows — use a scan to find
  out what yours reports.
