Read and control Mastervolt equipment (battery blocks, chargers, inverters, displays) from
ESPHome over a CAN transceiver.

MasterBus is a closed protocol running at 250 kbit/s on CAN. The wire format used here was
recovered by watching a live installation and checking every step against the vendor's own
library. Nothing in this component guesses at a frame it has not seen.

> [!WARNING]
> This component can write to the bus, and on a real installation those fields open battery
> contactors and switch chargers. Read [Writing to the bus](#writing-to-the-bus) before you
> configure a `switch`, `button` or `number`.

## Setup

The component sits on top of the standard [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) component, so any CAN
platform ESPHome supports will do.

### A configuration to start from

You do not need to know anything about your bus to begin. This is complete: paste it, set the two
pins, flash, and read the log. It transmits one broadcast, lists every device that answers, and
prints the configuration for what it finds.

```yaml
esp32:
  board: esp32dev
  framework:
    type: esp-idf

logger:
  # The canbus component logs one line per frame. A busy MasterBus carries about 200 a second,
  # which is far more than a serial log can carry, so mute it.
  logs:
    canbus: WARN

canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    id: mb_can
    tx_pin: GPIO5     # to the transceiver's TX / D
    rx_pin: GPIO4     # to the transceiver's RX / R
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
    rx_queue_len: 32

masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
  scan: true
```

Turn `scan` off again once you have what you need. Then add `devices:` and the entities the scan
printed.

If your equipment is one of the products listed under
[Configurations for known equipment](#configurations-for-known-equipment), you can take the entity
blocks from there instead and only change the addresses.

### Configuring the CAN bus

```yaml
canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    id: mb_can
    tx_pin: REPLACEME
    rx_pin: REPLACEME
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
    rx_queue_len: 32
```

- **bit_rate** (**Required**): MasterBus runs at **250 kbit/s**. No other rate will work.
- **mode** (*Optional*): `NORMAL` lets the node transmit, which is what polling and writing need.
  Use `LISTENONLY` for a first look at a bus you would rather not touch; entities still update
  from what other devices ask for.
- **rx_queue_len** (*Optional*): a busy MasterBus carries about 200 frames a second. A queue of 32
  or more keeps frames from being dropped during a slow loop.

> [!NOTE]
> The `canbus` component logs one line per frame at DEBUG. At 200 frames a second that is far more
> than a 115200 baud serial log can carry. Add `logs: {canbus: WARN}` to your
> [Logger](/components/logger/) unless you are specifically debugging the bus.

### Using an external CAN controller

Not every board has a CAN peripheral. ESPHome offers exactly two platforms, and which one you can
use is decided by your MCU:

| Platform | Interface | Works on |
|---|---|---|
| [`esp32_can`](/components/canbus/esp32_can/) | built in | the ESP32 family only |
| [`mcp2515`](/components/canbus/mcp2515/) | SPI | anything ESPHome runs on |

There is **no I2C or UART CAN platform** in ESPHome. If your board has no CAN peripheral, SPI and
an MCP2515 is the route.

The MCP2515 is a full CAN 2.0B controller, so it handles everything MasterBus needs: 250 kbit/s is
available on all four supported crystal frequencies, and extended identifiers, which every
MasterBus message uses, are supported in both directions.

```yaml
spi:
  clk_pin: GPIO18
  mosi_pin: GPIO23
  miso_pin: GPIO19

canbus:
  - platform: mcp2515
    id: mb_can
    cs_pin: GPIO5
    clock: 8MHZ       # must match the crystal on your module
    bit_rate: 250kbps
    mode: NORMAL
    can_id: 0
```

Three things catch people out:

The `clock` must match the crystal actually fitted to your module, not what the listing said.
Boards ship with 8 MHz and 16 MHz crystals and look identical; the part is marked on the can. Get
it wrong and the bit rate is wrong by the same ratio, which looks like a dead bus rather than like
a misconfiguration.

Most cheap MCP2515 modules are 5 V parts. They pair the controller with a TJA1050 or MCP2551
transceiver and expect 5 V logic. On a 3.3 V MCU the SPI lines need level shifting, and the
transceiver itself will not run from 3.3 V. Modules sold as 3.3 V, or ones where you replace the
transceiver with an SN65HVD230, avoid the problem.

The module already contains a transceiver. The [Hardware](#hardware) section below is written
around wiring a bare transceiver to an MCU's own CAN pins; with an MCP2515 module that part is done
for you. Everything from [The connector](#the-connector) onwards still applies: termination,
grounding and the connector pinout do not care which controller you chose.

> [!WARNING]
> The MCP2515 holds only two received frames. ESPHome polls it from the main loop rather than
> using its interrupt pin, and empties both buffers on each pass. On a quiet bus that is ample. A
> MasterBus carrying around 200 frames a second delivers one every five milliseconds, so two slots
> is a thin margin against a main loop that can take ten milliseconds or more when something else
> is busy, and anything arriving while both are full is lost. By comparison `esp32_can` buffers in
> software, and `rx_queue_len: 32` holds well over a hundred milliseconds of the same traffic.
>
> Lost frames degrade freshness rather than break anything: a missed answer is asked for again on
> the next `update_interval`. How much it matters in practice has not been measured here.

### Configuring the hub

```yaml
masterbus:
  id: mb
  canbus_id: mb_can
```

- **id** (*Optional*, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): the hub's ID, so entities can
  reference it.
- **canbus_id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): the
  [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) component to listen on.
- **address** (*Optional*, int, default `0x500001`): the address the hub speaks as, in its node
  requests and after a write to an event. The default is the vendor library's own. Give a second hub on the
  same bus an address of its own; it needs a device class in its top five bits, like every
  MasterBus address, and must not be one of your devices'.
- **scan** (*Optional*, boolean, default `false`): scan the bus at boot: ask every device to
  announce itself and print what each one answers, for the converter. To scan only when you ask,
  use the [`masterbus.scan`](#masterbus-scan-action) action instead. See [Finding your
  equipment](#finding-your-equipment).
- **log_all_frames** (*Optional*, boolean, default `false`): log every MasterBus frame at DEBUG,
  with the message type and address already decoded. For protocol work, not for normal use.
- **node_request_interval** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time), at least `1s`):
  ask every device on the bus to announce itself this often. A device only announces itself when a
  node asks, and an announcement keeps a device online even when nothing polls it. Where another
  node on the bus already asks, such as the vendor's own software, this is not needed. Off by
  default.
- **on_unknown_frame** (*Optional*, [Automation](/automations)): fires for any frame this
  component reads past without understanding: a message type it cannot name, or a write that
  another node sent. See [`on_unknown_frame`](#on_unknown_frame).
- **devices** (*Optional*, list): the equipment you want to talk to. See below.

Several hubs may be configured, one per CAN bus.

### Configuring devices

Every entity belongs to a device, and a device is one physical unit addressed by the identifier it
announces on the bus.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_battery
      device: 0x6D56EA
      timeout: 60s
```

- **id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): referenced by entities through
  `masterbus_device_id`.
- **device** (**Required**, hex int): the device's MasterBus address, `0x0` to `0x7FFFFF`. The
  extended CAN identifier gives the address 23 bits; the rest carries the message type. The top
  five bits are the device's `class`, and the other eighteen are worked out from the serial and
  article numbers on its label, so a unit keeps its address for life. A scan reads it off the bus.
- **timeout** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time), default `60s`): how long
  without a frame before the device counts as offline and its entities go unavailable. A device
  says nothing unless it is asked, so where this hub is the only node polling one, its timeout has
  to outlast the slowest `update_interval` among its own entities. ESPHome warns when it does not.
- **on_online** / **on_offline** (*Optional*, [Automation](/automations/actions/)): see
  [Automations](#automations).

## Finding your equipment

You do not need to know your addresses in advance. Set `scan: true`, flash, and read the log, or
start a scan whenever you like with the [`masterbus.scan`](#masterbus-scan-action) action:

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  scan: true
```

The scan sends a node request, then asks every device that answers what it is and walks every one
of its groups on all four tabs, reading each field's number, display type, name, unit, range,
factory setting and whether it takes a write, and for a list field what each entry is. It reports
what each device answered, one line per device, one per group, one per field and one per list
entry:

```txt
[I][masterbus]: Scan found device 0x6D56EA
[I][masterbus.scan]: Walking 8 devices across all four tabs for their fields.
[I][masterbus.scan]: device device=0x6D56EA class=27 eventables=2 article="66025000" serial="AB00A0001" name="BAT 1" revision="AA0012" firmware="1.4 0.33"
[I][masterbus.scan]: count device=0x6D56EA q=0x0B value=260
[I][masterbus.scan]: count device=0x6D56EA q=0x40 value=117
[I][masterbus.scan]: count device=0x6D56EA q=0x41 value=119
[I][masterbus.scan]: group device=0x6D56EA tab=0 group=1 name="Battery"
[I][masterbus.scan]: field device=0x6D56EA tab=0 group=1 param=136 display=1 name="State of charge" unit="%" min=0 max=100 step=1 factory=0 writable=0 eventable=0
[I][masterbus.scan]: field device=0x6D56EA tab=0 group=1 param=139 display=1 name="Battery" unit="V" min=0 max=52.8 step=0.01 factory=0 writable=0 eventable=0
[I][masterbus.scan]: field device=0x6D56EA tab=0 group=1 param=117 display=5 name="Close relay" unit="" min=0 max=1 step=1 factory=0 writable=1 eventable=1
[I][masterbus.scan]: group device=0x6D56EA tab=3 group=0 name="Device"
[I][masterbus.scan]: field device=0x6D56EA tab=3 group=0 param=26 display=3 name="Language" unit="" min=0 max=5 step=1 factory=0 writable=1
[I][masterbus.scan]: option device=0x6D56EA tab=3 param=26 index=0 name="English"
[I][masterbus.scan]: option device=0x6D56EA tab=3 param=26 index=1 name="Nederlands"
```

Copy those lines into the converter below to get a configuration out of them. Log prefixes and
timestamps do not need trimming off first.

The lines carry only what the device reported: `tab` and `display` are the vendor's own numbers,
and a key is simply absent where the device did not answer for it. A field with no `display` at
all is one the device would not describe, and the converter leaves it out rather than guessing.

The device line says what the equipment is. `article` is the article number on its label,
`serial` the serial number of that one unit, `revision` its revision code, `firmware` the software
version of each of its processors (a battery block has two), and `name` is the name the device
carries, the one its display shows, which an installer may have chosen: the six identical battery
blocks on the installation this component was developed against answer `BAT 1` to `BAT 6`.
`class` is the kind of equipment, which a MasterBus device carries in the top five bits of its own
address, so it is there for every device, asked or not:

| `class` | Equipment | | `class` | Equipment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `2` | inverter | | `19` | alternator battery monitor |
| `6` | inverter/charger (Combi) | | `20` | display panel (MasterView, EasyView) |
| `10` | charger | | `24` | DC/DC converter |
| `11` | generator | | `25` | transformer |
| `12` | DC source | | `27` | battery monitor (shunts, and batteries with their own) |
| `14` | interface | | `28`-`30` | DC, AC and AC/DC measurement |
| `16` | DC switching | | `31` | system overview |
| `17` | AC switching | | | |

`eventables` is how many event slots the device has, which identical units share. The article
number is how you find your equipment under
[Configurations for known equipment](#configurations-for-known-equipment). Equipment that does not
carry one of these leaves its key off, and the line is printed either way, so a device that
describes no fields at all still reaches your configuration.

> [!NOTE]
> The serial number identifies your unit, and the line carries it. Take it out before pasting a scan
> somewhere public; the converter does not need it and leaves it out of the configuration.

`count` lines follow each device line: every count the vendor's own software asks a device
for, by its number `q`, with the value the device gave. Some point at a field: `0x40` plus n is
the field number of the device's eventable n. On a battery block `0x40` is field 117,
`Close relay`, and `0x41` field 119, `Open relay`, and the converter uses those to name an
event's entries. `0x22`, `0x24` and `0x25` are the field numbers of the device's language, clock and
calendar. The rest mean only what the vendor calls them (`0x0B` the software version, `0x1C`-`0x1F`
the versions of the device's menus, `0x20` and `0x21` its alarms) and are there for anyone working
out more of the protocol.

`option` lines follow a list field, one per entry, and a list holds as many entries as the
field's `max`. A radio or dropdown field's options come with their `name`; a device list's entries
with the `entry` device they name; an event command's with the `target` device the event is sent
to and the `eventable` number on it, which that device's `count` lines turn into a field. The
converter turns them into the option names a `select` or `text_sensor` shows: a display panel's
`Event 1 target` lists `BAT 1` to `BAT 6`, and its `Event 1 command` lists `Close relay` and
`Open relay`. A field whose device carries a longer form of its name adds
`long_name` (and `very_long_name`) where it says more than `name` does.

`writable` and `eventable` are the device's own word on what a field allows. A reading answers
`writable=0`, a setting `writable=1`, and a button both. The converter picks the platform by it: a
field the device calls writable becomes a `number`, `switch` or `select`, and one it calls
read-only stays a `sensor` or `binary_sensor`. `factory` is the field's factory setting, which the
converter notes beside a configuration-tab entity.

The group name matters, and the converter carries it through as a comment: field names on this
equipment are short and repeat. A battery block reports three separate fields all called
`Battery`: voltage, current and temperature. The converter tells a repeated name apart by what
the field measures (`Battery voltage`) and, where the same reading appears in two groups, by the
group.

The converter adds what a scan implies. A unit gives the `device_class` (voltage, current,
power, temperature, frequency), and a percentage from 0 to 100 on a battery monitor is a
battery's charge, which a display panel's backlight is not. Monitoring readings get
`state_class: measurement`, and their decimals follow the device's own step. Settings get
`entity_category: config`, and the rest of the configuration, alarm and history tabs `diagnostic`,
so Home Assistant files them away from the readings. A device's comment names its product as the
vendor's configuration tool shows it, by article number, and for equipment listed under
[Configurations for known equipment](#configurations-for-known-equipment) the converter takes that
section's names, intervals and notes for every field the scan matches.

A field number only means something together with its tab. Field 9 is `AC Input` current on a
MassCombi's monitoring tab; the same number on another tab is another field entirely.

All four tabs can be read. Alarm and history values travel on message numbers taken from the
vendor's own library and not yet watched on a bus, and the converter marks those entities with a
comment. See [Tabs](#tabs).

Where a device does not answer `writable`, as in a scan from before it was reported, the
converter offers the read-only platform of a pair (a `sensor` where a `number` may be possible, a
`binary_sensor` where a `switch` may be) and says in a comment what to change it to. A list field
becomes a `text_sensor` showing the entry by name, or a `select` where the device says it takes a
write, with the names the scan's `option` lines carry. A scan from before those lines gives it
numbered placeholders, in the device's own order, for you to rename.

> [!TIP]
> Scanning transmits, so it needs `mode: NORMAL`. On an installation of eight devices it reported
> 513 fields and 1137 list entries across all four tabs in about five minutes. It asks a device
> how many groups a tab holds rather than finding out by asking for one that is not there. Turn it
> off again once you have your configuration.

## Entities

### Common options

Every platform below accepts these, in addition to its own base schema:

- **masterbus_device_id** (**Required**, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): which device this
  field belongs to.
- **param** (**Required**, hex int, `0x0` to `0xFFFF`): the field number.
- **tab** (*Optional*, default `monitoring`): `monitoring`, `alarm`, `history` or
  `configuration`. See [Tabs](#tabs).
- **value_type** (*Optional*): how to read the 32 bits the device sends. Each platform accepts only
  the types it can render, and defaults sensibly, so you rarely need to set this.
- **update_interval** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time)): see [Polling and
  update_interval](#polling-and-update_interval). **Left unset the entity never transmits.**
- **timeout** (*Optional*, [Time](/guides/configuration-types#time)): how long without a value
  before this one entity goes unavailable. Unset, it follows its device. Use it for a field that
  changes rarely by nature, such as a relay, sitting beside fields that update every second.

### `sensor`

Numeric readings. Accepts `value_type` of `float` (default), `boolean` or `list_option`.

```yaml
sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_battery
    param: 139
    name: Block voltage
    update_interval: 10s
    unit_of_measurement: V
    device_class: voltage
    state_class: measurement
    accuracy_decimals: 2
```

A field that reports "not a number" publishes as unavailable rather than as zero.

### `binary_sensor`

A boolean field, read only.

```yaml
binary_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 21
    name: Charger state
    update_interval: 1min
```

### `switch`

A boolean field you can also set.

```yaml
switch:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 21
    name: Charger state control
```

The switch publishes nothing when you operate it. It follows what the device reports afterwards,
so it never shows a state the equipment did not confirm. Give it an `update_interval`, or make
sure something else on the bus polls the field, or it will never show a state at all.

### `button`

Fires an event field once. Buttons take no `update_interval`, as there is nothing to read back.

```yaml
button:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_battery
    param: 117
    name: Close relay
```

### `number`

A numeric field you can set. `min_value`, `max_value` and `step` are required; a scan prints the
device's own range as a comment.

```yaml
number:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 20
    name: Charger max current
    min_value: 0
    max_value: 100
    step: 1
    unit_of_measurement: "%"
```

### `select`

A list field that takes a write. The options are the device's own, listed in the order it numbers
them, because the reported index picks the entry; a scan reports their names. Accepts `value_type`
of `list_option` (default), `device_id` for a device list, or `eventable` for an event field.

```yaml
select:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_panel
    tab: configuration
    param: 10
    name: Panel auto lock
    options: ["Off", "2 minutes", "5 minutes", "10 minutes"]
```

> [!NOTE]
> Get the order from a scan, not from guesswork. An index the list does not cover publishes as
> unavailable and logs a warning.

### `text_sensor`

Accepts `value_type` of `text` (default), `time` or `date`, and for a list field that is read
rather than written, `list_option`, `device_id` or `eventable` together with `options`: the names
of the entries in the device's own order, as a scan reports them.

```yaml
text_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_charger
    param: 24
    name: Charger battery name
    update_interval: 5min
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_interface
    param: 0
    name: Interface state
    value_type: list_option
    options: ["Idle", "Communicating"]
    update_interval: 30s
```

A **text** field does not answer with text. It answers with the number of an entry in the device's
own string table, and the component reads that entry to get the string: two exchanges rather than
one. A field whose entry the device does not hold publishes as unavailable.

A **time** field counts seconds, and publishes as `hh:mm:ss`. The same field type serves both a
clock and a countdown (a shunt's `Time` is a time of day, its `Remaining` is a span of hours), so
the hour is not folded into a day and a long span reads as `160:52:00`.

A **date** field packs the calendar into one number, and publishes as `YYYY-MM-DD`.

A number that cannot be either, because it is negative or larger than the format holds, publishes as
unavailable and logs a warning, rather than being truncated into something that looks plausible.

If you want the number the device sent as well as the rendered text, declare the same field twice:
once as a `text_sensor` with `value_type: time`, and once as a `sensor` with `value_type: float`.

```yaml
sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_shunt
    param: 6
    name: Time remaining raw
    value_type: float
    update_interval: 5min

text_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_shunt
    param: 6
    name: Time remaining
    value_type: time
```

One answer feeds every entity configured for that field, and an entity that hears an answer it did
not ask for skips its own next poll, so the second entity costs no extra traffic. Give the
`update_interval` to one of them.

#### What a device says about itself

A `text_sensor` with `info` instead of `param` shows something the device says about itself
rather than one of its fields:

```yaml
text_sensor:
  - platform: masterbus
    masterbus_device_id: mb_battery
    info: serial_number
    name: Battery serial number
    entity_category: diagnostic
```

- **info** (**Required** without `param`): one of `article_number`, `serial_number`,
  `product_name`, `revision_code`, `firmware` or `class`.

The device is asked when it first answers, and again when its announcements say something about it
has changed, which is how the vendor's own tools notice. `firmware` lists each of the device's
processors' software versions. `class` asks nothing: it is the kind of equipment the device's
address already says it is (see [Finding your equipment](#finding-your-equipment)). A device that
does not carry one of these leaves the entity unavailable.

## Tabs

A MasterBus device organises what it exposes into four tabs, and `tab:` picks which one an entity
reads. The same field number means different things on different tabs, so the tab is part of a
field's address, not a filter.

| Tab | Read | Write |
|---|---|---|
| `monitoring` (default) | yes, measured | yes, measured |
| `configuration` | yes, seen | yes, from the vendor library |
| `history` | yes, from the vendor library | yes, from the vendor library |
| `alarm` | yes, from the vendor library | yes, from the vendor library |

**Measured** means frames of that shape were captured on a live bus and checked against what the
vendor's own software reported for the same field. **Seen** means answers of that shape came back
on a live bus and read sensibly (a display's own name, its message texts, a battery's charge
thresholds) but were not compared with the vendor's software. **From the vendor library** means the
message numbers are the ones the vendor's own library uses for that tab, read out of the library
itself, but no device has yet been watched answering one. Every tab carries a value the same way, a
field number and 32 bits, and an answer that is not that length is dropped rather than guessed at.
A wrong assumption therefore shows up as a value that never arrives, not as a wrong reading.

Writing works the same way on every tab: the value goes out on the tab's own request number, which
is how the vendor's own software writes. Only monitoring writes have been watched on a bus. The
configuration tab is where a write changes a charger's voltage or a battery's limits, so check a
setting against the equipment's own display after the first write to it.

## Polling and `update_interval`

A MasterBus device answers a field **only when asked**. Nothing arrives unprompted, so an entity
with no `update_interval` never transmits and stays silent. That is deliberate, so that a first
configuration on someone's boat or battery bank does nothing until asked.

The unusual part: **an answer meant for someone else counts.** MasterBus answers are broadcast, so
a display panel or another bridge asking the same device for the same field updates your entity
too. When that has already happened within the interval, the request is skipped.

So `update_interval` is an upper bound on how stale a value may get, and an upper bound on your own
traffic, not a fixed transmit rate. On a bus where a display panel already polls at 1 Hz, a node
configured with 35 entities at `10s` was measured transmitting **nothing at all** for the fields the
panel covered, while every entity kept updating. For a field nobody else wants, the same node polls
at exactly the configured interval.

## Writing to the bus

Writing works, and on real equipment it does real things. Three properties of the protocol are
worth knowing before you wire a button into an automation.

First, what goes on the wire: **a write to an event is two frames.** The value goes to the field,
and then the hub's own address goes to the next field number, recording which node set the event
off, the way the vendor's own software fires one. Any other write is the value alone: there the
next field number is a field of its own, such as a display panel's `Page duration` right after its
`Backlight`. The hub speaks as `0x500001`, the vendor library's own address, unless given an
`address` of its own.

The first write to a field asks first. A field says whether it takes a write and whether it is
an event (its `writable` and `eventable` in a scan), and before the first write to each field the
hub asks both; a field that says it takes no write is not written, and the log says so. A device
that does not answer within half a second is written anyway, and a field that does not say it is
an event is written as none.
After a `switch`, `number` or `select` writes, the field is read back 200 ms later, so the entity
shows what the device kept.

There is often no state to read back. Fields like `Close relay` and `Open relay` are *events*:
firing one sends the command, and reading the field afterwards always returns false. It reports the
last command, not the contactor's position. The only evidence a command took effect is some other
field, such as the block's own current.

A write that reaches the bus is not a command that took effect. Equipment can be shared between
devices, and then only one of them accepts the command for it. On the installation this component
was developed against, two battery blocks share one contactor: either one opens it, but only one of
the two closes it again. Correctly formed writes to the other block changed nothing at all, with no
error anywhere.

A field that takes a write can still ignore it. Nothing comes back from a write to say whether
it was acted on, so read the result: the read-back above, or another field. A wrong `param` on a
field that takes writes fails silently.

This component therefore does not retry, does not publish optimistically, and does not decide which
device to address on your behalf. Which device owns what is a property of your installation, not of
the protocol. The read-back shows what the device kept; build any retry logic you need on top.

## Automations

### `on_online`

Fires the first time a device answers after being offline.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  devices:
    - id: mb_battery
      device: 0x6D56EA
      on_online:
        - logger.log: "Battery bank answered"
      on_offline:
        - logger.log: "Battery bank stopped answering"
```

### `on_offline`

Fires when nothing has been heard from the device for its `timeout`.

### `on_unknown_frame`

Fires for any frame this component reads past without understanding. There are two kinds:

- **A message nobody here reads.** Either a type the vendor's own library would refuse, or one it
  accepts without looking inside: the bus message, `0x02`, and the bootloader's, `0x3D`, `0x1D` and
  `0x2D`.
- **A write that another node sent.** A write uses the same message as a read, six bytes instead of
  two, and a write to an event is followed by a second write to the next field number that carries
  the writer's address. This hub never hears its own frames, so a write seen here came from somewhere else: a
  device carrying out one of its events, or a button pressed on a display panel. A value answer of
  any length other than six bytes is reported the same way.

Reads and their answers, and the walks a display makes through a device's menus, are what a bus
carries all day, and they never fire it.

It sits on the hub rather than on a device, and deliberately: the frames worth catching here come
from equipment that may never have been declared, which is exactly what a device-level trigger
cannot see.

```yaml
masterbus:
  canbus_id: mb_can
  on_unknown_frame:
    - logger.log:
        format: "undecoded message 0x%02X from device 0x%06X, %u bytes"
        args: ["type", "device", "x.size()"]
```

Three values are available: `x` is the payload as a `std::vector<uint8_t>`, `type` is the six-bit
message type, and `device` is the 23-bit address in the frame. For a write that is the device
written to; the second frame of the pair carries the writer's address as its value.

This is an instrument, not a feature. The only way to learn where an unnamed message sits, or what
an event does on the wire, is to catch one. On a bus where nothing but polling happens it stays
quiet.

If yours does fire, the lines it logs are worth reporting: turn on `log_all_frames` alongside it,
note what the equipment was doing at the time, and open an issue with both. A frame line can be
pasted into a test exactly as it was logged, so a message type somebody else's equipment sends can
be decoded without that equipment being on hand.

If you are trying to catch something rare on equipment you cannot sit and watch, push it somewhere
that keeps it rather than writing it to a log:

```yaml
    - homeassistant.event:
        event: esphome.masterbus_unknown
        data:
          type: !lambda return format_hex(type);
          payload: !lambda return format_hex_pretty(x);
```

Nothing is compiled in unless you configure the trigger.

### `masterbus.scan` Action

Starts a scan: the same one `scan: true` runs at boot, at any time you choose. A button in Home
Assistant is the usual way to start one.

```yaml
button:
  - platform: template
    name: Scan MasterBus
    on_press:
      - masterbus.scan:
```

- **id** (*Optional*, [ID](/guides/configuration-types#id)): the hub to scan with. Needed only when
  there is more than one.

The scan asks the devices to announce themselves, waits ten seconds, and then walks every device
that answered, which takes minutes on a real installation. Started again before that wait is over,
it starts the wait afresh; started while a walk is running, it logs a warning and does nothing.
Using the action compiles the scan in without `scan: true`, so a configuration can keep it at hand
without scanning on every boot.

## Configurations for known equipment

Field numbers are not standard across Mastervolt equipment. The same number means different

A configuration is only valid for the product it was taken from. If yours is not listed, scan it:
the scan prints blocks like these for whatever answers.

several devices share one hub. Mixing equipment means one `masterbus:` block with each device

### Adding a product

The list grows by contribution, and an entry needs three things:

1. **A scan of the one device**, pasted as it was logged: its `device` line, and the `group` and
   `field` lines that follow it. Those carry the field numbers, names, units and ranges, which is
   most of an entry already: the converter turns them into the YAML block.
2. **What the equipment is**, as its label or manual names the model. The scan's `device` line
   gives the article number and the name the device carries, but that name is often one an
   installer chose, such as `BAT 1`, rather than the model.
3. **Whether it was run.** Say whether the configuration was flashed and watched against the
   equipment's own display, or only rebuilt from a recording. An entry that was never run is still
   worth having, and readers need to know which kind they are looking at.

Open a pull request against the documentation with those three, or an issue with the scan pasted
into it if you would rather someone else wrote the section. Keep the entity names, device classes
and `update_interval` values in the style of the sections below rather than the converter's
defaults: a scan cannot know that field 139 is a voltage, and a reader copying an entry should get
something that looks right in Home Assistant without editing it.

## Hardware

Everything here is about getting an MCU physically onto the bus. None of it is specific to
MasterBus's use of CAN. It is the same job as putting a board on any 250 kbit/s CAN segment, with
one complication: the connector carries power as well, and Mastervolt does not publish its pinout.

In order:

1. Get the parts: [What you need](#what-you-need).
2. Plug the breakout into a spare MasterBus port and identify the conductors:
   [The connector](#the-connector), then [Checking the pair](#checking-the-pair).
3. Decide whether the board may share a ground with the bus:
   [Grounding and isolation](#grounding-and-isolation).
4. Take the terminating resistor off the transceiver module: [Termination](#termination).
5. Wire the transceiver to the MCU and to the bus: [Wiring the transceiver](#wiring-the-transceiver).
6. Flash [the configuration to start from](#a-configuration-to-start-from) and read the scan.

### What you need

- **A CAN transceiver with 3.3 V logic.** The MCU's CAN peripheral gives you logic-level TX and RX;
  the transceiver turns that into the differential pair. An **SN65HVD230** or **TJA1051T/3** works
  directly with an ESP32. Avoid the **MCP2551** and similar 5 V-only parts: their logic levels do
  not match a 3.3 V MCU without level shifting.
  If you are using an [MCP2515 module](#using-an-external-can-controller) you already have one on
  the board and can skip this; check which transceiver is fitted, because the common modules pair
  the controller with a 5 V part.
- **A way into the bus.** MasterBus uses RJ45 connectors and daisy-chains between devices. An
  **RJ45 male breakout board** is the least invasive route: it plugs into a spare port on a
  MasterBus hub and brings the eight conductors out to screw terminals, with nothing cut or
  soldered.
- **A multimeter.** Not optional, see below.

### The connector

A MasterBus cable is an ordinary straight-through Ethernet patch cable in Mastervolt's own jacket
colour: eight conductors in an RJ45 plug, with the usual Ethernet colour code inside. Four of them
matter to a board on the bus.

| Pin | Colour (T568B) | Carries | Connect to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | orange/white | CAN_H | transceiver `CANH` |
| 2 | orange | CAN_L | transceiver `CANL` |
| 3 | green/white | ground | transceiver `GND` |
| 4 | blue | +12 V | nothing, unless you [power the board](#powering-the-board) from it |
| 5 | blue/white | +12 V | nothing |
| 6 | green | ground | |
| 7 | brown/white | not connected | |
| 8 | brown | not connected | |

The colours are those of a cable wired to T568B, which nearly every patch cable is. A cable wired to
T568A has the orange and green pairs exchanged, so go by the pin number when in doubt.

The table was measured on the installation this component was developed against: the CAN pair and
its polarity on pins 1 and 2, and 12 V on the blue pair. An unrelated installation documented in the
[masterbus-esp32](https://github.com/pmarches/masterbus-esp32) project found the same pinout, and
pins 1 and 2 are also where CiA 303-1, the usual pinout for CAN on RJ45, puts CAN_H and CAN_L.

### Checking the pair

> [!CAUTION]
> Check the table above against your own cable before you connect anything. Mastervolt does not
> publish the pinout, and the cable carries **+12 V** two pins away from the CAN pair. Wiring a
> transceiver to the power pins destroys it.

With the installation **powered down**:

1. Measure resistance between conductors, pair by pair. The CAN pair reads about **60 Ω**, two
   terminators in parallel at the ends of the chain. Nothing else on the connector will read that.
2. Power the bus back up and confirm with a voltmeter. The supply pair sits at about **12 V**.
   CAN_H and CAN_L both idle near **2.5 V** relative to the bus ground, moving a volt or so apart
   when traffic flows.

If the 60 Ω pair is not on pins 1 and 2, trust the meter and not the table.

### Wiring the transceiver

<Figure
  src={masterbusWiringImg}
  alt="Wiring diagram of a MasterBus RJ45 breakout connected to an ESP32 through an SN65HVD230 CAN transceiver"
  caption="Pins 1, 2 and 3 of the MasterBus connector go to the transceiver; the rest stay unconnected."
  layout="constrained"
/>

Three connections to the MCU, two to the bus, and the standby pin:

| Transceiver | Connects to | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `CTX` / `TXD` | MCU CAN transmit pin | |
| `CRX` / `RXD` | MCU CAN receive pin | |
| `VCC` | MCU 3.3 V | not 5 V, for an SN65HVD230 |
| `GND` | MCU ground **and** MasterBus ground, pin 3 | see [Grounding](#grounding-and-isolation) |
| `CANH` | MasterBus CAN_H, pin 1 | the pair you measured |
| `CANL` | MasterBus CAN_L, pin 2 | |
| `Rs` / `S` | **ground** | high-speed mode |

Then name the two MCU pins in your `canbus:` block:

```yaml
canbus:
  - platform: esp32_can
    tx_pin: GPIO20   # to CTX
    rx_pin: GPIO21   # to CRX
    bit_rate: 250kbps
```

On an ESP32 the CAN controller can be routed to most GPIOs, so pick any free pair, but avoid the
chip's strapping pins, and anything already taken by flash, PSRAM or USB. The reference setup uses
an **ESP32-C6** with GPIO20 and GPIO21.

> [!WARNING]
> The `Rs` pin is the single most misleading fault on these modules. Held high it puts the
> transceiver in standby, where the **receiver still works and the driver does not**. The node then
> reads the bus perfectly and every transmit fails with `ERROR_ALLTXBUSY`, which looks exactly
> like a dead chip or a software bug. Tie `Rs` to ground.

### Termination

A CAN segment is terminated with 120 Ω at each **end**, and nowhere else. MasterBus is a daisy
chain that Mastervolt already terminates at both ends with its own terminator plugs, so a board
tapping into a spare hub port sits on a **stub** and must not add a terminator of its own.

Most SN65HVD230 breakout modules ship with a 120 Ω resistor fitted. Leaving it in place makes
three terminators: the bus drops from about 60 Ω to about 40 Ω and starts throwing form and stuff
errors. Remove the resistor, or open the module's jumper if it has one.

Check with everything powered down, measuring across CAN_H and CAN_L:

| Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ~60 Ω | Two terminators. Correct. |
| ~120 Ω | One terminator. One end is unterminated. |
| ~40 Ω | Three. Remove yours. |

> [!NOTE]
> If it is missed, the symptom is not obvious: the controller initialises cleanly and then either
> receives nothing or goes bus-off.

### Grounding and isolation

The transceiver needs a ground reference shared with the bus. Whether that is safe to make
directly depends on your installation.

Measure it. With everything powered down, measure the resistance between MasterBus 0 V and the
ground of anything else the MCU touches: another CAN bus, a USB host, a mains-powered supply.

- **Bonded (below about 1 Ω):** the grounds are already common. A bare transceiver adds no new
  path, and an SN65HVD230 is fine.
- **Not bonded:** connecting them creates a new current path between two systems that were
  separate, which on a battery installation can mean a ground loop carrying real current. Use an
  **isolated** transceiver such as an **ISO1042**, with an isolated supply for its bus side.

If the MCU also sits on a second CAN bus, such as an inverter link, check that pair too. Two bare
transceivers on one board bond both buses to each other through the MCU's ground.

### Powering the board

The MasterBus cable carries about 12 V, and it is tempting to run the MCU from it. That takes a
step-down converter from 12 V to the board's own supply voltage; no ESP32 board takes 12 V on its
3.3 V pin. Two things to weigh before you do:

- It is the installation's supply, fused and budgeted for MasterBus devices. An ESP32 with WiFi
  draws short peaks of a few hundred milliamps, which is small but not nothing.
- A separate supply avoids that entirely, but then the grounding question above becomes the one you
  have to answer.

Either way the transceiver's ground must reference the bus.

## Troubleshooting

Messages below are the ones the component and the CAN layer actually print, so you can search for
what you are looking at.

### `send to extended id=0x... failed with error 2!`

The name behind error 2 is `ERROR_ALLTXBUSY`, and it is misleading: the ESP32 driver reports it for
every transmit failure, not only for a full queue. Read it as "the send did not happen", then work
out which of two very different faults you have. **What separates them is whether anything is still
being received.**

*Values keep arriving, only sending fails.* The receive path works and the transmit path does not.
Check the transceiver's `Rs`/standby pin, then swap the transceiver: this exact symptom has turned
out to be a module fault.

*Nothing arrives either.* The controller has gone bus-off. It enters that state after about thirty
consecutive failed transmissions of one frame, roughly twenty milliseconds, and then disconnects
itself from the bus entirely, so reception stops too and every entity goes unavailable. **Nothing
in ESPHome brings a controller out of bus-off**: recovery needs an explicit call, an asynchronous
wait and a second call, and none of them happen. Only a reboot restores it.

A channel that was never wired looks identical: a controller transmitting where nothing can
acknowledge it reaches bus-off in milliseconds and stays there. If a channel is silent in both
directions, suspect the wiring before the driver.

### `Scan heard nothing. Check wiring, bit rate and termination`

Nothing at all is being received. Check the bit rate is `250kbps` first. It is the single most
common cause and the symptom is total silence rather than corruption. Then measure 60 Ω across
CAN_H and CAN_L with the bus powered down. Then the wiring.

### `Wrote field N but could not send the frame that follows it`

The value went out to an event but the frame that must follow it (the hub's address, written to
the next field number) did not, so the device may have taken the write, ignored it, or half-applied
it. Read the field back before assuming anything. This is a transmit failure like any other: see
error 2 above.

### `Could not put the node request on the bus`

The scan could not transmit, so it falls back to the announcements other nodes' requests bring
in. A device only announces itself when something asks, so on a bus where a display panel or
another bridge is polling you will still find equipment, and on a bus where this node is alone you
will find nothing. Scanning needs `mode: NORMAL`.

### `Scan is full at N devices`

More devices answered than the scan can hold. The rest are not listed. Raise the limit or scan a
smaller bus.

### `Maximum number of esp32_can components created already`

More `esp32_can` blocks than the chip has CAN controllers. An ESP32-C6 has two; most other ESP32
variants have one. Use an [MCP2515](#using-an-external-can-controller) for a further bus.

### `canbus took a long time for an operation`

The frame drain and everything it triggers ran longer than the loop allows. A verbose log over a
slow serial port is the usual cause: the logger blocks until the port accepts the bytes. Mute the
frame log, lower the log level, or read the log over the API.

### Entities stay unavailable

Either the device is not answering (check the address against a scan) or nothing is asking. An
entity with no `update_interval` transmits nothing and only updates when something else on the bus
asks for the same field.

### One entity goes unavailable while the rest of its device is fine

It has a `timeout` of its own and the field has not been answered within it. Either the field
changes more rarely than the timeout allows, or nothing is polling it.

### A write is accepted but nothing happens

See [Writing to the bus](#writing-to-the-bus). Check whether another device shares the equipment
you are commanding. A field that takes no write at all is refused before anything is sent, with
`Field N of device 0x... takes no write, by the device's own word; not written` in the log.

### The log is missing lines or is full of half-lines

The serial log cannot keep up with a busy bus. Mute the frame log with `logs: {canbus: WARN}`,
raise the baud rate, or read the log over the API instead. Two readers on the same serial port also
produce interleaved half-lines that look like corruption but are not.

## Limitations

- Only `monitoring` reads and writes have been watched on a bus. The other tabs use the message
  numbers of the vendor's own library. See [Tabs](#tabs).
- A list field answers with the index of an entry, and the entry's name is in your configuration,
  as a scan reported it. A device whose list changes after the scan shows the old names.
- A scan reads a device's article number, serial number and name and whether each field takes a
  write; entities do not consult them at run time. A device's status here comes
  from whether it answers, not from what it reports: the vendor's own library has no other source
  either, and reports every device it has found as on.
- A CAN controller that goes bus-off never recovers on its own. This is a property of the
  underlying [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/) platform rather than of this component, but it is what
  a MasterBus node looks like when it dies: every entity unavailable, the bus itself healthy, and
  only a reboot fixes it. See [Troubleshooting](#troubleshooting).
- Firmware update over MasterBus is out of scope, and the bootloader tab is deliberately absent.
- The component carries no table of device types, article numbers or field layouts, and never
  infers one. What a field means is a property of your equipment. The configurations above are
  documentation of what has been observed, not something the component knows. Use a scan to find
  out what yours reports.

## Resources

Mastervolt publishes no protocol documentation, and there is no specification to link to. What
exists is other people's measurements, and this component's own.

- [pmarches/masterbus-esp32](https://github.com/pmarches/masterbus-esp32): packet captures from an
  unrelated installation, with a Wireshark dissector. Its identifier decode is not the one used
  here, but the captures are real traffic from equipment this project does not have, and they are
  what confirmed three of the message numbers documented above.
- `masterbus_protocol.h` in the component's own source. Every constant carries how far it is
  actually known, marked VERIFIED, LIBRARY or UNVERIFIED, together with what established it. It
  is the authority when this page and the source disagree.
- [CAN Bus](/components/canbus/): the layer underneath, and where bit rate, mode and queue lengths
  are really configured.
